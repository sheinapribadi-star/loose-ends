What is this person trying to finish?

Pinterest replaced a keyword-clustering pipeline with one model that reads a person’s activity and writes a short list of their goals, like Modern Farmhouse Kitchen. The part I find most interesting is a line from the post: once you collapse the pipeline, the prompt becomes the ranking policy and the safety policy. So each sentence in the prompt is a product decision. This page lets you switch them off one at a time and see what each one was protecting.

      Your policy

      The first four slots are the ones a notification could use. A slot counts as useful when it names a real goal, for the first time, in more than one word. Hover or focus a slot to mark its lines in the log.

      What would show it works

      What the post reports

      Against the clustering system, the model’s journeys lifted email click-through 1.1% and push opens 1.3% in a US and Canada test, and a multilingual rollout replicated the direction. The earlier clustering version had already beaten interest-based notifications by 88% on email clicks.

      The metric I would own

      The Yes rate on the “Are you interested?” module the post describes in journey emails, read by slot position. If the policy is right, slots one to four should earn Yes about equally; a sharp drop after slot two would suggest filler. Guardrails: No rate, unsubscribes, and the share of people who get an empty list, which should rise a little, not a lot.

      Why a prompt edit is a launch

      Every clause above moves real slots for at least one of three people. That is the post’s point about giving prompts the same review discipline as model code. Offline, I would rerun the evaluator’s support score on a fixed sample for every prompt change, the way the useful-slot count changes here.

      How it works

      1. The log uses the post’s format: events grouped by date and action, pins separated by semicolons, tags by commas. Gray tags are auto-generated ones like home decor or art.
      2. The reading step is done by hand. Each pin carries two hand-written labels: the goal it belongs to and a narrower facet (cabinets, countertops). With “one goal, one journey” off, slots are built from facets instead, which is how fragmentation shows up.
      3. Scoring. Search and save count 5, comment 3, like 2, click 1. Recency halves weight every 90 days. The post gives the order of actions, not these numbers; they are mine. Ties go to the more recent journey.
      4. Drops. One-off means support on a single day. “Mostly dislikes” means more hides than positive actions and no search or save. Names are deduped case-insensitively, first one wins, capped at twelve.
      5. Naming. With the specificity rule off, a journey is named by its most frequent keyword, auto-tags included, which is how “Recipes” and “Crypto” happen.
      6. Support check. A plain word match against positive activity, with light plural handling. Words like making or beginner are treated as framing. It is a floor, not a judge: the post uses a separate language model for this.
